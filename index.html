Thirteen challenges

Every submitted project competes for the open challenges; nobody has to nominate. For the pitched challenges we hand teams the problem and they build the answer; a team picks up to three on the submission form. Any topic, any community: a Muslim-community angle is welcome where the challenge asks for one and never required where it doesn't.

Prizes

Parked until the challenge list is settled. Working assumptions, open to change:

  • Each challenge carries its own prize, the way a Devpost prize page reads.
  • Gift cards per team member rather than hardware; teams are one to four people and a monitor can't be split.
  • "Most Likely to Become a Venture" carries the MC² Accelerator slot: desk time, a mentor, a follow-up demo day.
  • Budget to be set by the committee once the number of challenges is fixed.

Workshops

Most high-school teams have never used git, and submissions require a public repo. Short, practical sessions so every team arrives at the deadline with a repo and a submission.

Git and GitHub from zero

How the weekend moves through the app

  1. Before the event. Info pages are live; submissions are closed.
  2. Submissions open at Saturday kickoff. Teams draft, edit and submit.
  3. Submissions close about an hour before pitches, automatically, even if nobody clicks. The gallery and pitch order appear.
  4. Judging. Teams pitch in random order; judges score on their phones.
  5. Deliberation. Judges see the combined results and decide the awards.
  6. Winners announced. Badges appear in the gallery and on the Prizes page.
ParticipantsNo accounts. A QR code and an event code get them in.
  • Scan the QR code in the room and enter the event code. No sign-up, no password.
  • Read the challenges, prizes, rules, schedule, judges, resources and announcements on their phones.
  • One submission per team in Devpost format: team and members, project name, one-line tagline, story (including a "Who we talked to" section), tools used, repo link, demo and video links, up to five screenshots.
  • Opt in to up to three pitched challenges and tick a box if the project includes hardware. Every project is automatically considered for all nine open challenges.
  • Save as a draft, submit, and keep editing until the deadline. A private edit link lets any teammate make changes; organizers can reissue a lost link.
  • After the deadline, browse every team's project in the gallery, filtered by challenge or hardware. Winners show a badge.
JudgesScore each pitch on a phone as it happens.
  • Log in with their email and a password the organizers hand over.
  • The screen jumps to the team on stage when the MC moves to the next pitch. A manual picker is there as a backup.
  • See the project summary, links, screenshots, pitched challenges and hardware badge.
  • Score five criteria from 0 to 10, each with a short guide: technicality, originality, practicality, aesthetics, wow factor. Add private notes. Everything saves as they go.
  • Finalize each team when done, or skip a team they have a conflict with.
  • See only their own scores until deliberation, so no judge is swayed by another.
MC on the floorRuns the pitch order from a phone.
  • Shuffle the pitch order and adjust it if a team needs to move.
  • Tap "now pitching" to move every judge's screen to the next team.
  • Cannot see scores, results or anyone's contact details.
Deliberation and awardsThe app ranks; the judges decide.
  • Scores are adjusted per judge so a strict judge and a generous judge count equally.
  • An overall leaderboard, plus a shortlist for every challenge sorted by what that award is about: Most Technically Complex by technicality, Most Innovative by originality, Ihsan by aesthetics, Most Impactful by practicality.
  • The hardware shortlist shows only hardware projects; pitched-challenge shortlists show the teams that opted in. A challenge with one entrant can still be awarded.
  • Judges' private notes are visible to the whole panel during deliberation.
  • Shared places are allowed. A setting controls whether one team can win several awards: allow, warn (default) or block. The venture award doesn't count toward the limit.
  • Publishing the winners adds badges in the gallery and updates the Prizes page.
OrganizersEverything else, from one admin area.
  • Move the event between phases; set the open and close times.
  • Post announcements and edit challenge wording.
  • Add judges, MCs and admins; generate and reset their passwords; disable an account.
  • See every submission with contact details; edit or delete entries; reissue edit links; give one team a few extra minutes past the deadline.
  • Watch a grid of which judge has scored which team.
  • Rotate the event code if it leaks, and print the QR code.
  • Export submissions, contacts, scores and results to a spreadsheet; erase contact details after prize follow-up.
Trust and privacyFair results, protected data.
  • The deadline is enforced twice, by the app and by the database, so nothing slips in late.
  • Emails and Discord handles are visible to organizers only, never in the gallery or judge screens.
  • Screenshots are checked, resized and stripped of location data, then stored privately.
  • Daily backups during the event week, plus an export right after the deadline and after awards.

Pitching: one room, every judge sees every pitch

  • Fair comparison. Every score comes from the same judges watching the same field. With two rooms, each judge sees half the teams, and a team in the stronger room scores lower against tougher company. Score normalization can't fix that, because no judge saw both halves.
  • Open awards need full coverage. Nine of the thirteen challenges are decided across every project. Judges can only argue those out if they all saw every pitch.
  • Teams watch each other. For first-time hackers, seeing everyone else's project is a big part of what brings them back.
  • One of everything. One MC, one screen, one Wi-Fi zone, one schedule.
  • The cost is time. Ten-minute slots for 8 to 13 teams is 80 to 130 minutes. The MC keeps it moving on the floor by trimming Q&A when needed; judges score during changeover.

Left out

  • Google sign-in for judges; admin-generated passwords instead
  • Registration, which stays on the Summit's existing page
  • Emails from the app; announcements and the event chat carry updates
  • Video uploads; teams share YouTube, Loom or Drive links
  • Audience voting and certificates

Timeline and fallback

  • Fallback first: a Google Form for submissions and a printed scoring sheet per judge, ready before any code.
  • App complete Oct 20, dry run with helpers on their phones Oct 21–22, content frozen Oct 23.

What we need to decide

  • Which challenges run, their names and wording, and the open-to-pitched ratio.
  • Whether a team can win more than one award, or each team takes at most one so recognition spreads across the room.
  • Whether MC² Labs supplies any components for the Hardware Hack, or teams bring their own.
  • Prize budget, and whether MC² commits an Accelerator slot for the grand prize.
  • Who runs the git and pitching workshops and who helps in the room.
  • Six judges confirmed by name, and one coordinator who is not a judge.
  • Whether younger Summit divisions can enter the hackathon section; every problem has a simpler version if so.
